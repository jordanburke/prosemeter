I'd push back — that turns a suite people ignore into a suite that lies, which is worse.

Your diagnosis is right, though. A red suite nobody trusts gives us nothing, and I'm glad you want to fix it. The problem is the filter. "Failed twice this month" doesn't select for flaky tests. It selects for tests that touch real behaviour — the ones covering timing, concurrency, external calls, and the parts of the system most likely to actually break. Delete by that rule and we'd systematically remove our best coverage while keeping the tests that assert a getter returns what we set. The suite goes green and tells us less than it did before.

Some of those failures are also real bugs that only show up under load or ordering, and we'd be deleting the only thing that catches them.

Here's what I'd do instead — same goal, week or two of work:

1. **Quarantine, don't delete.** Move the offenders to a separate job that runs but doesn't block merges. CI goes green today, and the coverage stays.
2. **Put a name and a date on each one.** An owner and a two-week deadline. No owner after a week means it goes in the delete pile with an issue explaining what stopped being tested.
3. **Sort them before fixing.** Real flakiness — shared state, `sleep`-based waits, unmocked network, test-order coupling — is usually four or five root causes across dozens of tests. Fix a cause and a batch goes green at once.
4. **Delete what's genuinely worthless.** Tests asserting implementation details, duplicates, tests for code we removed. That's a real category and I'd cut it happily — just on the merits, not on a failure count.
5. **Stop the regression.** Any test that enters quarantine twice gets rewritten or deleted, decided by a person.

The part I fully agree with: shipping past a red build is the actual danger, and every week we tolerate it costs us more than the tests are worth. Let's do the quarantine today so CI is honest by tomorrow, then work the list down.
