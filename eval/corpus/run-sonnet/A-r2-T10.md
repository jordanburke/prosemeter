I get the goal — nobody should be ignoring a red CI — but deleting the tests is the wrong fix, and I'd push back on it.

A test that fails intermittently is telling you one of two things: either the test is flaky (bad setup, timing, shared state), or it's catching a real bug that only shows up under certain conditions — a race condition, a timing issue, an ordering dependency. Deleting it doesn't resolve either case. It just removes your only signal that the problem exists. The bug, if there is one, ships to production instead of failing in CI. That's a worse outcome than an occasionally-red pipeline.

There's also a selection problem with "failed twice in the last month": that threshold catches the exact tests most likely to be guarding against a real race condition, since those are the ones that fail unpredictably rather than consistently. You'd be pruning out your flakiest — and often most important — coverage.

What I'd do instead:

- **Quarantine, don't delete.** Move flaky tests to a separate job that doesn't block merges but still runs and reports. That gets CI green for the team immediately without losing the coverage.
- **Track them somewhere visible** — a label, a dashboard, a ticket per flaky test — so quarantine doesn't become "delete it and forget it" six months later.
- **Triage each one**, not the whole batch at once. Some are genuinely a bad `sleep()` or shared fixture and take ten minutes to fix. Some point at a real concurrency bug worth a ticket and real time.
- **Set a policy going forward** — new flaky tests get quarantined within a sprint, not left to rot, so the backlog doesn't just rebuild itself.

Same end result — a green pipeline the team trusts — without throwing away the tests that might be the only thing standing between you and a bug like the one in T3.
